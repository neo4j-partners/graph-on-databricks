## Core message per slide

**Slide 1: Enterprise AI initiatives are failing**
Most enterprise AI initiatives are failing, and the cause is not the model. Agents hallucinate because they reason over raw tables without knowing what those tables actually mean, and that missing grounding is why the large majority of AI pilots never reach production. The Neo4j Enterprise Knowledge Layer solves this. It gives agents a business lens on the data so they understand what the raw data means, and it supplies the semantic and relational context that makes them accurate and auditable. That is what Neo4j enables in the Databricks ecosystem, built from three things: ontologies, data, and memory.

**Slide 2: Enterprise Agents also Need a Graph based Knowledge Layer**
Those three pillars are built on a graph. Ontologies are the business domain model, mapped to the physical data through a semantic map that records where the data lives, whether the lakehouse, the graph, or a source such as BigQuery, alongside business rules and actions. Data is the physical reference and domain data, including virtual graph data materialized from the lakehouse. Memory holds long-term and short-term agent memory, procedural memory of how the business operates, and the context graph of agent decision traces. The layer lives on Neo4j and plugs directly into the Databricks agentic ecosystem.

**Slide 3: Supplier Risk, Dual Data Architecture**
The knowledge layer is used in a dual data architecture. The lakehouse on the left holds the core instance tables plus a gold layer of write-back tables. The Neo4j graph on the right is split into an instance graph, where a supplier supplies a customer, belongs to a business unit, and a customer has invoices, and a knowledge layer on top that governs and classifies it. Governing defines what a supplier means and what rules apply. Classifying runs graph algorithms to mark a supplier high risk and uses similarity to flag customers approaching risk before they cross the threshold, an early warning system. Those results write back to the gold tables so they query as standard lakehouse queries. Two bridges connect the sides. The Spark connector is the data bridge: in the medallion pattern raw data lands in bronze, is cleansed into silver, and the connector writes silver rows into Neo4j as nodes and relationships; after graph algorithms run, results return as Spark DataFrames for batch and stream jobs. The second bridge, for agents at query time, is the Neo4j MCP servers, which inspect the graph schema and run read queries against the knowledge layer, including natural language to Cypher.

**Slide 4: Unity Catalog Semantics**
That is the Neo4j side. To put it to work it meets how Databricks already organizes meaning, which Databricks calls Unity Catalog Semantics, the grouping of Genie services. Genie One is the enterprise chat and the primary consumption layer. Its scope is controlled by domains, governed tags on tables, pipelines, and documents, and by metrics, business definitions such as how net revenue is calculated. Genie One draws on Genie Ontology, auto-mined from tables and queries, and Genie Agents, a curated agent scoped to a governed slice of tables. Domains and metrics together form a flat domain model: tagging plus one governed formula per table.

**Slide 5: Flat tags plus knowledge layer, together**
The limitation of domains and metrics is that they are just flat tagging. The Neo4j knowledge layer adds a rich graph structure over the same data, where a customer holds a contract that is subject to a price posting rule. The two combine through a bi-directional sync: a domain tag such as contract syncs into a contract entity in the knowledge layer, and metric formulas sync alongside it, then extend with business rules and meaning. The result is one model, the flat governed tags and metric formulas from Databricks plus the business rules and relationships from Neo4j, giving complete governance over the same data.

**Slide 6: From Question to Auditable Answer**
Combined, they go from a question in Genie One to an auditable answer. For the question "For a Florida contract, is a price posting required, and what net_revenue is at risk?", Genie One first grounds in Genie Agents and Genie Ontology, applying the finance domain and the net_revenue metric. It then queries Neo4j through the Neo4j MCP servers to discover the knowledge layer and how its rules apply, and the MCP servers also let the agent convert natural language to Cypher. The answer comes back grounded and auditable: Florida requires filing the posting seven days prior, with the path it took, the rule it applied, and the metric it used.

**Slide 7: Live demo**
A credit analyst asks which customers to investigate next, before they default rather than after. From the lakehouse alone, Genie can only rank on failures already on the books, overdue balances and booked defaults, because no definition of an early warning exists there. Connect the Neo4j knowledge layer and, asked for the governed term, Genie resolves the Risky Customer definition, its rule, and its threshold from the graph. The grounded result: clean accounts, none defaulted or yet delinquent, whose payment behavior already resembles the customers who have failed, found by a graph algorithm against a governed cutoff so the same accounts surface every run.

## Refined speaker script

**Slide 1**
Most enterprise AI initiatives are failing right now, and the cause is not the model. Agents hallucinate because they reason over raw tables without knowing what those tables actually mean, and that missing grounding is what keeps the large majority of AI pilots from ever reaching production. The Neo4j Enterprise Knowledge Layer solves this. It gives your agents a business lens on the data, and it supplies the semantic and relational context that makes them accurate and traceable. That is what Neo4j enables in the Databricks ecosystem, and it is built from three things: ontologies, data, and memory.

**Slide 2**
This Enterprise Knowledge Layer is built on a graph and organized as three pillars. Ontologies are your business domain model, mapped to the physical data through a semantic map that records where the data lives, whether in the lakehouse, the graph, or another source such as BigQuery, alongside the business rules and actions. The data is your physical reference and domain data, including virtual graph data materialized from the lakehouse. And memory holds your agents' long-term and short-term state, the procedural memory of how your business operates, and the context graph that captures agent decision traces. This layer lives on Neo4j and plugs directly into the Databricks agentic ecosystem.

**Slide 3**
Bringing that knowledge layer into the Databricks ecosystem is what makes the two better together, in a dual data architecture: the Databricks lakehouse plus the Neo4j knowledge layer. On the left is the standard lakehouse, the core instance tables plus a gold layer of write-back tables. On the right is the Neo4j knowledge graph. The bottom is the instance graph, where a supplier supplies a customer, a supplier belongs to a business unit, and a customer has invoices. On top sits the knowledge layer of business terms and entities that governs the instance graph, defining what a supplier is and what rules apply to it. This is the business lens on your data. It also classifies the instance graph. I can run graph algorithms to mark a supplier as high risk, and use similarity to flag customers approaching risk before they cross the threshold rule, an early warning system. Those classifications write back to the gold tables so they can be queried as standard lakehouse queries. There are two bridges between the lakehouse and the knowledge graph. The first is the Neo4j Spark connector, the data bridge. In the medallion architecture, raw data lands in bronze, is cleansed into silver, and the connector writes those silver rows into Neo4j as nodes and relationships. After graph algorithms run, the results are read back into Databricks as Spark DataFrames, which lets you run batch and stream Spark jobs against the graph. The second bridge is for agents at query time: the Neo4j MCP servers, which let a Databricks agent inspect the graph schema and run read queries against the knowledge layer, including natural language to Cypher. That is the bridge the Genie services use, and I will come to it next.

**Slide 4**
This dual data architecture works alongside how Databricks organizes meaning, which it calls Unity Catalog Semantics, the grouping of Genie services. At the top is Genie One, the enterprise chat and the primary consumption layer. Its scope is controlled by domains, which are governed tags on your tables, pipelines, and documents, and by metrics, which are your business definitions such as how net revenue is calculated. Genie One draws on Genie Ontology, an automatic context layer that extracts business knowledge from tables, queries, dashboards, pipelines, documents, and connected apps, organizes it into a living graph scored by authority with OntoRank, and uses it to ground Genie answers in verified enterprise context. It also draws on Genie Agents, manually curated, domain-specific AI query rooms. Each one behaves like a SQL-proficient domain expert on the dataset you curate for it. Taken together, domains and metrics are a flat domain model: tagging plus governed metric formulas.

**Slide 5**
The limitation of domains and metrics is that they are just flat tagging. The Neo4j knowledge layer adds a rich graph structure over the same data, where a customer holds a contract that is subject to a price posting rule. The two combine through a bi-directional sync. A domain tag such as contract syncs into a contract entity in the knowledge layer, and metric formulas sync alongside it. From there you extend those tags and metrics with business rules and meaning. The result is one model: the flat governed tags and metric formulas from Databricks, plus the business rules and relationships from Neo4j, giving complete governance over the same data.

**Slide 6**
Put those together and you can go from a question in Genie One to a traceable answer. Take the question, for a Florida contract, is a price posting required, and what net_revenue is at risk. Genie One first grounds the question in Genie Agents and Genie Ontology, applying the finance domain and the net_revenue metric. It then queries Neo4j through the Neo4j MCP servers, discovering the knowledge layer and how its rules apply to this question. The MCP servers let the agent convert natural language to Cypher. The answer comes back grounded and traceable: Florida requires filing the posting seven days prior, and you can see the path it took, the rule it applied, and the metric it used.

**Slide 7, live demo**
Let's walk through a scenario a credit analyst faces every day: deciding which customers to investigate next, before they default rather than after. You will see the same business question asked twice: first against the lakehouse alone, where the agent can only rank the customers already in trouble, and then with the Neo4j knowledge layer connected, where it answers from a governed definition and points at the clean accounts heading that way. Step by step.

*Step 1, the knowledge graph in Neo4j.* Start with how a business term is defined in the graph. This is where the business decides what risk actually means. A Risky Customer, for example, is a governed business term, defined by an explicit business rule and a threshold the business owns. The customers shown here were classified against that definition by a graph analytics job, which applies the rule and its threshold and writes the result back onto each customer. So an analyst sees not just who is flagged, but the exact rule that flagged them. That definition lives here, in the knowledge layer, the business meaning the graph adds on top of the lakehouse data.


*Step 2, the lakehouse alone.* Let's start the investigation by asking a Genie agent that is setup with the customer and invoice tables. I ask the natural question a credit reviewer would ask: which customers should we review next as most likely to default, and why. It comes back with a clean, plausible answer, but look at what it ranked on: overdue balances and booked defaults, the failures that have already happened. It is looking backward, at the accounts that have already failed, where the loss is booked and the credit team already knows. It has no definition of an early warning to reach for, because none exists in the lakehouse.

*Step 3, connect the Neo4j MCP server.* To bring the knowledge layer into Databricks, I created an MCP server connection to the Neo4j knowledge layer. Once it is defined, both my Genie agent and Genie One can use it.

*Step 4, Genie One.* Now Genie One, the enterprise chat, where I bring the two sources together: the Genie agent for the lakehouse facts, and the MCP server for the knowledge layer.

*Step 5, add the MCP connection.* Inside Genie One, under connections, I select the MCP server. Now it can reach the knowledge layer alongside the lakehouse data.

*Step 6, the reasoning.* Now I ask the grounded question, naming the term: which customers have Risky Customer status, and why. It takes a moment, so I ran it ahead. In the thought process you can see what it did: it read the graph schema, found the Risky Customer term with its rule and threshold, pulled the classified accounts, and cross-referenced all of it with the Databricks tables from the Genie agent.

*Step 7, the grounded answer.* And here is the grounded, reproducible answer: a set of customers classified as Risky Customer, and crucially, none of them have defaulted and none are even delinquent yet. These are clean accounts whose payment behavior already resembles the customers who have failed. The graph found them by running a graph algorithm, k-nearest-neighbours, which finds for each customer the others whose payment profile most closely matches theirs, then flags those whose closest matches are mostly already-delinquent accounts. The cutoff is a governed threshold read straight off the graph, not a number the model guessed, so the same customers surface every run. That is the difference: the lakehouse ranked the accounts that already failed; the graph, grounded in an authored definition, pointed at the accounts you can still act on before they do.

**Closing, bring it together**
The thread through all of this is grounding. Large organizations need a shared vocabulary, one agreed definition of what a supplier is, what makes a customer risky, how net revenue is counted, so that everyone and every agent means the same thing. Neo4j holds that shared vocabulary as a knowledge layer, along with the data and memory behind it; Databricks holds and governs the facts; the two sync into one model and meet at query time through the MCP servers. What we want is for Genie One to tie all of it together. Enterprise data lives in different forms, the lakehouse tables, the graph, the governed definitions, and a complete answer needs access to all of them. That is why the same Genie went from ranking the accounts that already failed to naming the clean ones heading that way, the same answer every run. Meaning resolved once in the shared vocabulary, reused by every agent. That is what lets a company build enterprise agents that bring together all the data across the enterprise, and it is what turns a stalled pilot into an answer a risk committee can act on.
